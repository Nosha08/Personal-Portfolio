Three Lessons I Learned from the Staten Island Chamber of Commerce

From Dr. Leslie’s “Who would care about that?” to a mattress salesman who ran his entire business on data science — three lessons from YEA and the Chamber.

Three weeks into the Young Entrepreneurs Academy, I came to Dr. Leslie with my business idea: a history-based board game. Her first question was “Who would care about that?”

The lecture hall went silent. I wasn’t prepared to pitch an idea I thought of on the bus ride there. She called me out. That embarrassment forced my hand.

I intensively researched other board games on The Game Crafter, ran demo game sessions during AP World, begged freelancers from Vietnam to Slovakia to design my prototype, and traded daily email chains with her. That became the most stressful twelve weeks I could ask for.

But with that, I created Ace of Ages. For the first time, I produced something tangible, something that I could even pitch in front of hundreds of people. Watching her face when I placed nationally said it all. It was just tough love. And I really did need it after all.

Brian the Mattress Salesman

My introduction to the Staten Island Chamber of Commerce came through the Young Entrepreneurs Academy, a 16-week startup accelerator. What stuck with me, however, wasn’t the curriculum. It was my mentor Brian, a mattress salesman with over 20 years of experience in the business.

Despite my initial skepticism of how he could help me, Brian caught my attention when he shared that he had no storefront, operating entirely mobile across the borough. When I bluntly asked how he marketed himself that way, his answer surprised me: data science. He leveraged data analysis and SEO to identify hot spots for sales, dictating where he’d set up shop each day. At that point, who was I to question his approach?

This interaction pushed me to join the Chamber as an intern and later part-time employee, driven to meet more “Brians” — entrepreneurs who were redefining traditional businesses through technology.

Hitting My Limits

Over two years at the Staten Island Chamber of Commerce, I managed our CRM system, coordinating data on 700+ member businesses. As congestion pricing emerged as a critical issue for local businesses in 2024, I led our data analysis efforts to quantify its impact. Through conversations with business leaders across sectors and industry-level segmentation, I projected a $130 million economic burden on Staten Island businesses. My analysis became integral in the Chamber’s advocacy materials submitted to the Governor’s office.

But I quickly hit my limits. When Chamber leadership tasked me with scenario modeling — hypothesizing the effects of vehicle type pricing adjustments or exemptions for deliveries in certain industries — I realized that my CRM-exported Excel sheets couldn’t answer these questions. I envisioned models that could simulate a variety of policy alternatives in real-time and personalized expense calculators for businesses, where they could dynamically input their Manhattan delivery schedules.

What I came to realize was that I understood the insights that would rally policymakers and stakeholders, but lacked the technical expertise to build scalable analytical tools. This gap between identifying problems and implementing tangible engineering solutions became the defining limitation of my work.

That awkwardness pushed me to undergo a journey in creative leadership: how to take ownership of a problem you didn’t create, refine an existing idea, and envision its larger impact.
