May 2026 By Noah Rozin Capstone Reflection

It Is 4,000 B.C.

A retrospective in the language I’ve always thought in — Civilization, civilizations, and the turn I’m about to take.

It is 4,000 B.C. — Turn 1

Lush hills, meandering streams, steam erupting from geysers.

This is the kind of map you’d kill for in a game of Civilization VI. Trust me, I’d know. As an unapologetic geography and history fanatic who can conjure obscure facts like Liechtenstein boasting the world’s largest denture industry, this was my forte. Civilization represented my chance to build an empire and my own legacy.

Throughout my life, I’ve been captivated by creating something bigger than myself. I remember seeing the grin illuminating my older brother’s face upon seeing his first 5-star review, selling the moon lamps he’d designed. He poured countless hours facing relentless interrogations from my dad over ‘wasting’ time. In that moment, seeing his work light up someone else’s room, it became worth it.

I’ve chased this feeling ever since, striving for my own 4,000 B.Cs. I founded an unofficial micronation, The Rotzinian Empire (ignore that I named it after myself); designed a history-based board game; and attempted to game the stock market. I came to love the satisfaction of building my own real-life civilizations. But eventually, you must choose which side of the stream you’ll settle on: by the sea or the mountains.

The Weight of a Thousand Eyes

For me, that question arrived after I was elected New York District Key Club Governor. As I walked on stage to deliver my acceptance speech, I felt the weight of nearly a thousand eyes, not just in the audience, but from every club and individual I now represented. It was overwhelming and exhilarating: a true 4,000 B.C. A chance to define the legacy of my civilization.

I spoke with conviction, but as I approached my speech’s defining moment, my District Administrator, Mr. Goldstein, cut me off. He stomped his foot, tapping his wrist impatiently. I spoke too long. Afterward, he echoed, “Any longer and we would’ve been here until your farewell speech.”

On the three-hour Greyhound ride home, I replayed every word, every pause, every turned head. What was once a symbol of my vision and ambition became a torn relic stuffed into the bottom of a drawer.

What I Kept Noticing

After two years of spearheading fundraising initiatives for the International Justice Mission and Thirst Project through Key Club, I began noticing a concerning pattern. The problems we were tackling were just symptoms of a larger, rooted disease: corruption.

My interviews with Matthew Okerblom and Andrew Reese, youth partnership coordinators from each organization, revealed the frustrating reality that it’s cheaper to save lives than to reform the systems that create victims. While these organizations were equipped to provide immediate relief, systemic corruption — costing $3 trillion annually — was the true unaddressed issue.

Through my work at the Staten Island Chamber of Commerce, managing CRM data on 700+ businesses, I’ve witnessed how data propels advocacy work. As congestion pricing emerged as a critical issue for local businesses in 2024, I led our data analysis efforts to quantify its impact — projecting a $130 million economic burden on Staten Island businesses. My analysis became integral in the Chamber’s advocacy materials submitted to the Governor’s office.

The Next Turn

Now, entering university, I’m on the brink of my next 4,000 B.C. But this time, I’m putting the strategy guide down. I’ve realized building an empire isn’t about executing Turn 1 perfectly; it’s about the hundreds of messy, unplanned turns that follow.

So when the next game asks whether to settle by the sea or mountains, I won’t agonize over the view. I’ll just make sure to ask who’s coming with me.
